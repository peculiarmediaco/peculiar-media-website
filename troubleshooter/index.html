MIXING

What are you working on?

Pick the part of the mix that's giving you trouble.

VOCALS

What's wrong with the vocal?

VOCALS / LEVEL

What kind of level problem are you hearing?

TRY THIS FIRST

Adjust the vocal level.

If the vocal is consistently too loud or too quiet, start with the simplest fix: change the fader level.

Compression is not a substitute for simply putting the vocal at the right overall level.

Before turning it up: Listen for something else masking the vocal. A guitar, keyboard, cymbal, or other element may be able to come down instead.

TRY THIS FIRST

Try volume automation.

If entire lines or sections move noticeably forward and backward, automation gives you direct control over those changes.

Bring louder sections down and quieter sections up. Then listen again before adding more processing.

Then consider compression: Compression can help control shorter level changes, but it doesn't replace a good overall level balance.

TRY THIS FIRST

Fix the problem words directly.

If only a few words or phrases jump out or disappear, you may not need to process the entire vocal more heavily.

Try clip gain or volume automation on those specific areas.

If it happens constantly: Gentle compression may help reduce the difference between louder and quieter moments.

VOCALS / BURIED

Does the vocal sound loud enough by itself, but disappear when the rest of the mix plays?

CHECK FOR MASKING

Something else may be competing with the vocal.

Instead of immediately turning the vocal up, listen for instruments occupying a similar range.

Guitars, keys, cymbals, and other midrange-heavy sounds can make a vocal difficult to hear even when the vocal itself is loud enough.

Try: Lower the competing element slightly, change its arrangement or panning, or make a small EQ adjustment where the conflict is strongest.

START SIMPLE

Bring the vocal up.

If the vocal is simply too quiet, moving the fader may be all it needs.

Don't add compression, saturation, or EQ just to solve a basic level problem.

VOCALS / HARSH

When does the harshness happen?

FIND THE SOURCE

Figure out where the harshness lives.

Sweep a narrow EQ band temporarily to help identify the frequency range that is bothering you.

Once you find it, return the EQ to neutral and try a small, broad cut rather than leaving a giant surgical notch.

Also check: microphone choice, microphone position, distortion or clipping, and any processing already making the top end brighter.

TARGET THE MOMENTS

Don't process the entire vocal for a few bad words.

If the harshness only appears occasionally, try automation or dynamic EQ on those specific moments.

A permanent EQ cut may make the rest of the vocal unnecessarily dull.

CHECK THE RECORDING

The louder performance may be changing the tone.

Check whether the microphone, preamp, interface, or recorded track is clipping when the singer gets louder.

If the recording itself is clean, dynamic EQ or automation can help control harsh moments without darkening the entire vocal.

VOCALS / MUDDY

Does the vocal sound muddy by itself, or only when everything else is playing?

CHECK THE SOURCE FIRST

Listen for excessive low-mid energy.

Microphone placement, proximity effect, the room, or the microphone itself may be contributing to the problem.

If you're still recording, try changing the microphone position before reaching for EQ.

If you're already mixing: Try a small reduction in the troublesome low-mid area. Use your ears rather than automatically cutting a predetermined frequency.

CHECK THE REST OF THE MIX

The vocal may not be the actual problem.

Guitars, keys, bass, room microphones, and effects can accumulate energy in the same area.

Bypass or lower competing elements one at a time and see whether the vocal suddenly becomes clearer.

Don't automatically cut the vocal. Sometimes removing a little from something else fixes the problem more naturally.

VOCALS / THIN

Are you still able to change the recording?

FIX IT AT THE SOURCE

Try changing the microphone position first.

Moving closer to many directional microphones can increase low-frequency response, while changing distance and angle can significantly alter the tone.

Try small position changes and listen before changing equipment or adding processing.

Also consider: microphone choice, the singer's position, and whether a high-pass filter is removing more low end than you intended.

CHECK WHAT'S MISSING

Don't immediately boost a pile of low end.

First check for aggressive high-pass filtering, previous EQ cuts, phase issues with layered vocals, or processing that has made the vocal overly bright.

If the vocal still needs more body, try a gentle tonal adjustment rather than a dramatic boost.

Context matters: A vocal that sounds slightly thin by itself may sit perfectly once the rest of the arrangement is playing.

VOCALS / SIBILANCE

Is the problem mostly S, SH, T, or similar consonants?

CONTROL THE SIBILANCE

Try a de-esser.

A de-esser reduces the vocal when excessive energy appears in the sibilant frequency range.

Set it so the distracting consonants become controlled without making the singer sound dull or unnatural.

For only a few problem words: Manual clip gain or automation may sound more natural than processing the entire performance.

THIS MAY NOT BE SIBILANCE

Go back to the harshness branch.

If the entire vocal sounds bright or painful rather than only the consonants jumping out, the issue is probably broader than sibilance.

Check microphone tone, EQ, saturation, distortion, and the upper-mid or high-frequency balance.

DRUMS

What's wrong with the drums?

DRUMS / KICK

What seems wrong with the kick?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Make sure you're starting with the sound you actually want.

If you're still recording, listen to the drum itself before trying to fix everything with EQ or compression.

Tuning, damping, the beater, microphone choice, and microphone position can all change the kick substantially.

Try this: Move the microphone and listen again. Small changes in position can change the balance between low-end weight, resonance, and beater attack.

CHECK THE REST OF THE MIX

Something may be covering it up.

If the kick sounds good alone but disappears when everything plays, don't assume the kick itself needs more processing.

Listen especially to the bass and other low-frequency-heavy parts. Try lowering competing elements temporarily and see whether the kick becomes easier to hear.

Also check: The part of the kick you need to hear may not only be the low end. Its attack can also help it remain audible on smaller speakers.

CHECK PHASE / POLARITY

Make sure the sources are helping each other.

Two microphones on the same drum capture the sound at slightly different times. When combined, some frequencies can reinforce while others cancel.

Listen to each source alone, then together. If the combined sound suddenly loses weight or punch, investigate the relationship between the tracks.

Try: Check microphone placement and timing, and compare the result with the polarity of one channel reversed. Keep whichever relationship actually sounds stronger and more appropriate for the recording.

DRUMS / SNARE

What seems wrong with the snare?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Start with the drum and microphone.

If you're still recording, check the tuning, damping, microphone position, and the sound of the snare in the room before reaching for processing.

Moving the microphone changes how much shell, head, attack, and surrounding kit it captures.

If you're already mixing: Check whether previous EQ, filtering, or other processing has removed some of the body or attack you actually need.

CHECK FOR MASKING

The snare may be competing with the rest of the arrangement.

If it works in solo but disappears in context, temporarily lower guitars, vocals, cymbals, or other competing elements and listen again.

You may find that making room around the snare works better than continually making the snare louder or brighter.

Use the whole drum sound: Overheads and room microphones may already contain part of the snare sound you're trying to create. Listen to them together before treating the close mic as if it has to do everything.

CHECK PHASE / POLARITY

Check how the two snare microphones combine.

A microphone above the snare and one below it capture opposite movements of the drumheads and can interact differently when combined.

Listen to the top mic, then the bottom mic, then both together. If the combined snare becomes noticeably thinner or loses impact, check their relationship.

Try: Reverse the polarity of one channel and compare. Don't do it automatically just because it's a bottom snare mic — listen and keep the setting that produces the result you want.

DRUMS / MUDDY OR BOOMY

Where does the problem seem to be coming from?

ISOLATE THE SOURCE

Find which drum is contributing the buildup.

Listen to the close mics one at a time and look for excessive resonance or low-mid buildup.

If you're still recording, tuning, damping, and microphone position may solve more than EQ will.

If you're already mixing: Reduce only what is actually causing the problem. Don't automatically carve low mids out of every drum just because the kit sounds muddy.

LISTEN TO THE ROOM

The microphones may be capturing something you don't want.

Overheads and room microphones capture much more than individual drums. The room itself, cymbals, low-frequency buildup, and microphone placement can all affect what you're hearing.

Temporarily lower those microphones and listen to what changes. If the muddiness clears up, you've isolated part of the problem.

Then decide: You may need less of those microphones, different filtering or EQ, or a different microphone position if you're still able to record again.

CHECK THE COMBINATION

The problem may only appear when everything adds together.

Several drum microphones can each sound fine alone while contributing too much of the same frequency range when combined.

Build the kit back up one microphone at a time and listen for the point where the unwanted buildup appears.

Also listen for: Phase relationships between microphones. If adding a microphone makes the kit unexpectedly hollow, weak, or strange rather than simply fuller, investigate how those signals combine.

DRUMS / CYMBALS

Where are the harsh cymbals coming from?

CHECK THE OVERHEAD BALANCE

Make sure the overheads aren't simply too loud.

Before reaching for EQ, lower the overheads and listen again. Sometimes the tonal problem is really a balance problem.

If the level feels right but particular cymbal frequencies still become painful, identify the offending area before making an EQ change.

Keep the rest of the kit in mind: Overheads may also be providing important snare, tom, and overall kit sound. Don't destroy everything else just to tame the cymbals.

CHECK THE BLEED

The cymbals may be reaching you through several microphones.

Soloing the overheads won't tell the whole story if the snare, tom, and other microphones also contain a lot of cymbal bleed.

Listen to those microphones together and identify which ones are adding the most unwanted cymbal sound.

Before aggressively gating or EQing: Remember that bleed is part of a live drum recording. The goal isn't necessarily to remove it — it's to keep it from hurting the mix.

FIX IT BEFORE THE MIX

Change what the microphones are hearing.

If the cymbals already sound unpleasant in the raw recording, consider the cymbals themselves, how they're being played, microphone choice, microphone position, and the room.

Moving an overhead microphone or changing its angle can alter the balance of cymbals and drums before any processing is involved.

If you can record again: Try fixing the source or placement before committing yourself to repairing the problem with EQ later.

DRUMS / SMALL OR LIFELESS

What feels like it's missing?

CHECK THE BIG PICTURE

Listen beyond the close microphones.

A kit built almost entirely from close microphones can sound very direct but may not contain much sense of the space around the drums.

Listen to any overhead or room microphones you recorded and see whether bringing some of that information back helps the kit feel larger.

If you don't have room microphones: Reverb can create a sense of space, but first decide what kind of space the recording actually needs rather than adding it automatically.

CHECK THE TRANSIENTS

Listen to the beginning of each hit.

The initial attack of a drum contributes heavily to how immediate and punchy it feels.

Compare the processed track with the raw recording. If the drums lost impact after compression or other processing, the processing itself may be part of the problem.

Try removing something first: Bypass processing one piece at a time. If the punch comes back, you've found something worth reconsidering.

BACK UP

Turn some things off.

Bypass your drum processing and compare it with the raw tracks. Then add each processor back one at a time.

Compression, EQ, limiting, saturation, gating, and other processing can all be useful, but several small changes can also accumulate into a drum sound that has less movement than the recording you started with.

You don't have to use something because you inserted it. If bypass sounds better, delete it and move on.

DRUMS / KIT BALANCE

What do you mean by “they don't fit together”?

LISTEN TO THE KIT

Stop mixing every microphone in isolation.

A drum recording is several microphones describing one instrument. A kick mic doesn't necessarily need to sound impressive by itself, and neither does a snare or tom mic.

Build the drum sound while listening to the microphones together. Overheads and room microphones can provide context that close mics don't have on their own.

Think kit first, microphone second. Solo is useful for finding problems. Context is where you decide whether you've actually fixed them.

CHECK THE MICROPHONE RELATIONSHIPS

Listen for cancellation between microphones.

Multiple microphones hear the same drum at different times and from different positions. When those signals combine, their relationship can change the tone substantially.

Add microphones one at a time and listen for a point where the kit suddenly becomes thinner, hollow, or loses low-frequency weight.

Then investigate: microphone placement, timing, and polarity. Don't automatically flip switches or line up waveforms — compare the results and keep what actually improves the sound.

START WITH LEVEL

Rebuild the balance before adding more processing.

If the cymbals, kick, snare, or another part of the kit dominates, start by adjusting levels rather than trying to EQ the imbalance away.

Bring the microphones down and rebuild the drum balance while listening to the entire kit.

Remember: The close microphones, overheads, and room microphones all contribute to the apparent level of each drum. The fader labeled “SNARE” isn't necessarily the only place your snare is coming from.

GUITAR

What's wrong with the guitar?

GUITAR / HARSH

When does it sound harsh?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Figure out whether the harshness is already in the recording.

Bypass any processing you've added and listen to the raw guitar. If the unpleasant tone is already there, start farther upstream.

If you're still recording, listen to the amp or instrument itself. Then check microphone position, microphone choice, amp settings, pedals, and anything else affecting the sound before it reaches the DAW.

If you're already mixing: Find the area that's bothering you and make the smallest useful adjustment. Don't automatically remove all the high end just because the guitar sounds bright.

CHECK THE MIX

The guitar may not be harsh by itself.

Several bright sounds playing together can make the whole mix uncomfortable even when none of them seems especially harsh in solo.

Temporarily lower or mute other guitars, cymbals, keys, or bright effects and listen for what actually changes.

Don't fix everything in solo. Identify which combination creates the problem before deciding which sound needs to change.

CHECK THE DISTORTION

Listen to where the fizz is actually coming from.

Distortion changes the harmonic content of the guitar, so the problem may be part of the tone itself rather than something created later in the mix.

If you can still change the source, compare different gain, amp, pedal, cabinet, or microphone settings before trying to repair it later.

If you're stuck with the recording: Compare the raw and processed guitar and identify the objectionable area by ear. A small tonal adjustment may be enough.

GUITAR / MUDDY

Is the guitar muddy alone, or only in the mix?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Find out where the excess weight is coming from.

If you're still recording, check the instrument, amp settings, microphone placement, cabinet, room, and effects before reaching for EQ.

If you're already mixing, bypass your processing and make sure you haven't created the problem yourself.

Then: If the recording still contains more low or low-mid energy than you need, reduce the problem area by ear rather than automatically using a predetermined guitar EQ curve.

LOOK AROUND THE GUITAR

The buildup may belong to the arrangement.

Bass, additional guitars, keyboards, vocals, reverbs, and other instruments can accumulate energy in similar ranges.

Mute or lower elements one at a time and listen for when the mix becomes clearer.

Ask before cutting: Does the guitar actually need less of something, or is another part occupying space it doesn't need?

GUITAR / THIN

Where did the size go?

START UPSTREAM

Check the sound before trying to manufacture body with EQ.

If you're able to record again, listen to the instrument or amplifier in the room and compare that with what the microphone is capturing.

Microphone position, microphone choice, amp settings, and the source itself can all change the apparent weight of the guitar.

Move something and listen again. A recording problem is often easier to solve before it becomes a mixing problem.

BACK UP

Compare your mix to the raw track.

Bypass EQ, filtering, compression, saturation, and other processing one piece at a time.

If the body returns when something is bypassed, reconsider whether that processor or setting is helping.

Especially check filters and EQ cuts. It's easy to remove useful information while trying to “clean up” a guitar.

CHECK THE RELATIONSHIP

Two good sounds don't always make a bigger sound.

If multiple microphones or recordings of the same performance sound full individually but thinner when combined, investigate their timing and phase relationship.

Try: Listen to each source alone and together. Change polarity where appropriate and compare. If you can still record, microphone placement may give you a better relationship at the source.

GUITAR / BURIED

Does it disappear only when everything else plays?

CHECK FOR MASKING

Find out what appears when the guitar disappears.

Instead of immediately making the guitar louder, mute or lower likely competitors one at a time.

Another guitar, keyboard, vocal, cymbal wash, or even effects may be occupying similar space.

Arrangement counts too. If several instruments are playing dense parts in similar registers, the cleanest solution may be changing what one of them plays.

START SIMPLE

Turn the guitar up.

If it is simply too quiet, fix the level first. You don't need an EQ trick to solve a fader problem.

Then listen again. If raising it creates a different problem, you've learned something useful about what it's competing with.

GUITAR / TOO BIG

What's taking over?

START WITH LEVEL

Turn it down.

It sounds obvious because it is. If the guitar is simply dominating the balance, lower it before trying to reshape it with processing.

FIND THE CONFLICT

The guitar may be occupying more space than the arrangement needs.

Listen for where it competes with the vocal, bass, other guitars, keys, or drums.

Then decide whether the answer belongs in level, arrangement, panning, or tone.

Don't automatically scoop the guitar. First figure out what you're trying to make room for.

CHECK THE EFFECTS

The guitar may not be what's taking up all that space.

Reverb and delay create additional sound around the original guitar. Temporarily mute the effects and listen again.

If the mix suddenly opens up, rebuild the effects balance from less rather than trying to EQ your way out of too much ambience.

GUITAR / MULTIPLE PARTS

What's happening when the guitars play together?

CREATE CONTRAST

Don't ask EQ to create differences that aren't there.

If you're still arranging or recording, try changing the part, register, chord voicing, instrument, pickup, amp sound, or playing style.

Two genuinely different parts are usually easier to separate than two nearly identical parts that have been heavily processed afterward.

CHECK THE ARRANGEMENT

Ask whether every guitar needs to be playing that much.

Mute one guitar at a time. If the song gets clearer without losing anything important, you may have found the simplest solution.

Try subtraction before separation. Fewer notes, different registers, different rhythms, or fewer simultaneous parts may solve the problem before EQ or panning does.

CHECK PHASE / TIMING

Figure out whether these are separate performances or the same source.

If multiple microphones captured the same performance, their timing and phase relationship can change the tone when combined.

If they are separate performances, small timing and tuning differences are normal and may be part of the sound rather than something that needs fixing.

Listen before correcting. Don't automatically align every waveform or edit every difference away. First decide whether the combined sound is actually a problem.

BASS

What's wrong with the bass?

BASS / CAN'T HEAR IT

When does the bass disappear?

START SIMPLE

Make sure this is actually a level problem.

Bring the bass up and listen again before adding processing. If it simply needed more level, you've solved the problem.

If increasing the level creates boominess, masking, or some other problem before the bass becomes clear, you've learned that level alone probably isn't the whole issue.

Also check the source: Make sure the recorded bass actually contains the character and definition you're expecting to hear.

CHECK FOR MASKING

Find out what makes the bass disappear.

Bring the rest of the mix back in one section at a time, or temporarily lower likely competitors.

Kick, guitars, keyboards, and other low or low-mid-heavy sounds can make the bass harder to distinguish even when its level seems reasonable.

Don't immediately boost the bass. First figure out what appeared at the same time the bass disappeared.

CHECK TRANSLATION

What part of the bass sound survives on the smaller speaker?

Small playback systems may reproduce less of the deepest bass than larger speakers or headphones.

Listen to whether the recording contains enough upper harmonic, attack, or midrange information to make the bass recognizable when the deepest part of the sound isn't reproduced as strongly.

Don't just add more sub-bass. If the playback system can't reproduce that information well, adding more of it may not solve the problem you're hearing.

LEVEL ISN'T THE WHOLE PROBLEM

Good. Turning it up taught you something.

If the mix gets heavier or muddier but the bass still isn't easier to follow, the part you need to hear may not be the part you're making louder.

Listen for what gives the bass its definition — attack, harmonic content, note clarity, or other information above the deepest low frequencies.

Now investigate: the source tone, competing instruments, and the balance of low-frequency weight versus definition.

BASS / TOO MUCH LOW END

Where do you hear the boominess?

CHECK THE SOURCE AND BALANCE

Find out whether the bass itself contains too much low end.

Listen to the raw track and compare it with your processed version. If the boominess was already there, look at the source. If it appeared later, inspect what you added.

Also lower the bass temporarily. A tonal problem can sometimes simply be a level problem.

Then make the smallest useful change. Don't remove the foundation of the bass while trying to control one part of its sound.

QUESTION THE ROOM

Make sure you're not fixing your monitoring instead of your mix.

Low frequencies interact strongly with rooms and listening position. If the bass sounds excessive where you mix but reasonable elsewhere, your room or monitoring position may be influencing what you're hearing.

Compare the mix on headphones and other playback systems, and move around your room while listening.

If the bass changes dramatically as you move: treat that as evidence that the room is part of the problem before making large changes to the mix.

DON'T FIX THE WHOLE TRACK YET

Find out why only certain notes are jumping out.

Compare the loud notes with the surrounding notes. The cause could be the performance, the instrument, the recording, processing, or what your room is emphasizing.

Listen on another playback system before assuming the problem exists in the recording itself.

If the problem really is in the track: targeted automation or dynamic control may be more appropriate than changing the tone of every note.

BASS / MUDDY

Is the bass unclear by itself or only in context?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Listen for what is obscuring the notes.

If you're still recording, check the instrument, playing technique, pickup or DI signal, amp tone, microphone placement, and effects.

If you're already mixing, bypass processing and compare it with the raw track.

Don't automatically scoop the mids. Some of the information that makes the bass understandable may live there too.

CHECK THE ARRANGEMENT

See what else is occupying the same space.

Lower guitars, keys, kick, effects, or other dense elements one at a time and listen for when the bass becomes clearer.

Ask: Does the bass need less of something, or does another instrument need to leave it more room?

BASS / INCONSISTENT NOTES

What kind of inconsistency are you hearing?

CHECK THE PERFORMANCE

You may not need to change the entire bass sound.

If only a few notes are too loud or too quiet, clip gain or volume automation can address those notes directly.

Compression can also reduce dynamic variation, but first decide whether the problem is occasional or happening throughout the performance.

A few bad notes don't necessarily require processing every note.

CHECK WHERE IT HAPPENS

Does the same thing happen outside your mixing room?

If certain notes consistently become much louder or quieter, compare them on headphones and other playback systems.

If the problem changes dramatically with your listening position, the room may be contributing.

If it follows the recording everywhere: investigate the instrument, performance, resonance, and whether targeted dynamic control would help.

BACK UP

Find the point where the balance changed.

Bypass your processing one piece at a time. Pay attention to compression, saturation, EQ, and anything else that responds differently to different notes.

If bypass fixes it, you've found your next question. You don't need another processor yet.

BASS / KICK

How are the bass and kick fighting?

CHECK THE COMBINATION

Find out what each source is contributing.

Listen to the kick alone, bass alone, and then both together. Lower one at a time and pay attention to which change actually clears the buildup.

You may need a different balance or tonal relationship, but first identify which source is contributing what.

Don't automatically cut both. Decide what job you want each one to do first.

FIND WHAT IDENTIFIES THE KICK

The kick doesn't have to win only with sub-bass.

Listen to the kick's attack and character as well as its low end. Then compare what the bass is occupying at the same moment.

Level, arrangement, tone, EQ, and dynamic control can all change the relationship.

Sidechain compression is an option, not step one. First understand what is actually disappearing and why.

LISTEN BETWEEN THE HITS

Figure out whether this is level, masking, or dynamics.

Listen to the bass immediately before, during, and after a kick hit.

If it only becomes difficult to hear during the kick, you've narrowed the problem considerably. Now compare their levels and tonal overlap before adding processing.

Preserve the groove. The goal isn't necessarily to make both equally obvious at every instant.

GIVE THEM DIFFERENT JOBS

Decide what you want to recognize about each sound.

If the low end is powerful but everything blends into one undefined mass, listen for the characteristics that distinguish the kick from the bass.

That distinction can come from arrangement, envelope, attack, harmonic content, level, or tone — not only EQ.

Make a decision before making a move. Which one should provide the deepest weight? Which one should provide more note or attack definition? There isn't one correct answer.

BASS / DI + AMP

What happens when you combine them?

CHECK TIMING / PHASE

The two signals may not be combining the way you expect.

A DI captures the signal directly while an amplifier microphone also includes the amp, speaker, microphone, distance, and room. Those paths do not necessarily arrive with the same timing or phase relationship.

Listen to each alone and then together. If the combination loses weight or changes dramatically, investigate their relationship.

Compare before correcting. Polarity, timing, and microphone placement are things to investigate — not switches to change automatically.

ASK WHAT EACH TRACK IS ADDING

You don't necessarily need all of both signals.

Listen to the DI and amp separately and identify what you actually like about each one.

If combining them simply adds more of the same information, try changing their balance before processing either one heavily.

And yes: you're allowed to mute one entirely if the other already does the job.

GIVE EACH ONE A REASON TO EXIST

What does each track contribute?

Maybe the DI gives you definition and consistency. Maybe the amp gives you character, distortion, space, or weight. Maybe it's the opposite.

Start with one track, then slowly add the other until it contributes something useful.

If you can't tell what the second track is improving, you may not need it.

FULL MIX

What's wrong with the mix?

FULL MIX / MUDDY

When did the mix become crowded?

CHECK THE ARRANGEMENT

There may simply be too much happening in the same place.

Mute instruments one at a time and listen for when the mix becomes clearer. Pay attention to parts playing similar notes, registers, rhythms, or sustained sounds at the same time.

Before reaching for EQ: Ask whether every part needs to be there, or whether changing an octave, voicing, rhythm, or arrangement would create the space naturally.

BACK UP

Find out what made it worse.

Bypass processing in stages. Start with mix-bus processing, then buses, effects, and individual tracks.

You're looking for the point where the mix becomes clearer again.

Don't add another processor yet. First identify whether something you've already added created or exaggerated the problem.

CHECK WHAT CHANGES

What enters the arrangement when the mud appears?

Compare a clear section with a muddy section. Look for new instruments, doubled parts, effects, denser performances, or changes in level.

If the problem only happens sometimes: you may not need a permanent fix. Automation or an arrangement change may solve the specific section without changing the entire mix.

FULL MIX / HARSH

Can you identify where the discomfort comes from?

ISOLATE IT

Make sure you've actually found the source.

Lower or mute the suspected instrument and listen to the full mix again. If the harshness disappears with it, you've narrowed the problem.

Now check the raw recording against the processed version to find out whether the tone came from the source or the mix.

Then fix that source. Don't darken the entire mix to solve one harsh instrument.

CHECK THE COMBINATION

Several reasonable sounds can add up to too much.

Cymbals, guitars, vocals, keys, distortion, and effects may all contribute energy in similar areas.

Lower groups of sounds and listen for when the mix becomes easier to hear.

Think accumulation. The answer may be several small changes rather than one enormous EQ cut somewhere.

CHECK WHAT YOU ADDED

Compare the processed mix with where you started.

Bypass EQ, saturation, compression, limiting, exciters, and other processing in stages.

If the mix becomes easier to listen to when something is bypassed, investigate that processor before adding another one to compensate for it.

FULL MIX / THIN

Was the mix always thin?

LOOK UPSTREAM

The mix may be reflecting the recording and arrangement.

Before trying to make everything bigger, listen to the individual sources and the arrangement.

Ask whether the performances, tones, instrumentation, and recordings actually contain the size you're expecting.

Mixing can shape what was recorded. It can't always manufacture an arrangement or performance that was never there.

YOU MAY HAVE CLEANED TOO MUCH

Start putting things back.

Compare the current mix with an earlier rough balance or bypass processing in stages.

Pay particular attention to filters, broad EQ cuts, aggressive dynamics processing, and anything applied repeatedly across many tracks.

“Cleaner” isn't automatically better. You may have removed information that was helping the mix feel substantial.

CHECK THE FOUNDATION

Listen to the bass and kick before changing the whole mix.

Mute everything except the low-frequency foundation and listen to its balance.

Then add the rest of the arrangement back gradually. You may discover that the low end isn't missing — it's being masked or overwhelmed.

Also check another playback system. Don't make large low-frequency decisions based on one room or one pair of speakers if you don't trust them yet.

FULL MIX / FLAT

What does “lifeless” mean here?

CHECK THE MOVEMENT

Not everything needs to stay equally loud.

Compare verses, choruses, transitions, and important moments. Look at your automation and dynamics processing.

If you've worked hard to make everything consistently audible, you may also have reduced some of the contrast that made the song move.

Try subtraction: instead of making the big moments bigger, see whether the quieter moments can actually be quieter.

CHECK DEPTH

Listen to what feels near and what feels far away.

Level, ambience, tone, performance, and stereo placement all contribute to perceived depth.

If every element has similar level and ambience, the mix may have very little front-to-back contrast.

Don't start by adding reverb to everything. First decide which sounds should feel close and which should feel farther away.

THIS MAY NOT BE A MIX PROBLEM

Listen to the arrangement before trying to automate excitement into it.

Compare sections and ask what actually changes: instrumentation, register, rhythm, density, performance, harmony, or intensity.

If nothing changes, that's useful information. The solution may belong in the arrangement rather than the mix.

BACK UP

Find where the energy disappeared.

Compare against your rough mix and bypass processing in stages. Listen especially to dynamics processing on buses and the mix bus.

The rough mix is evidence. If it makes you move and the processed version doesn't, don't assume the processed version is automatically more correct.

FULL MIX / TRANSLATION

What changes when you listen somewhere else?

QUESTION YOUR MONITORING

Compare before changing the mix.

Low-frequency reproduction varies greatly between rooms, speakers, headphones, cars, and small playback systems.

Compare several systems and look for a pattern. Don't chase every individual playback system independently.

If your mixing room is consistently the odd one out: investigate speaker placement, listening position, room behavior, and monitoring before repeatedly compensating in your mixes.

CHECK WHAT DEFINES THE PART

Why is the vocal easy to hear on one system and difficult on another?

Compare level, masking, ambience, and the tonal information that makes the vocal understandable.

Then compare what surrounding instruments are doing on the playback system where the vocal disappears.

Look for a pattern across systems. One strange speaker is an anecdote. The same problem on several systems is useful evidence.

LOOK FOR THE PATTERN

Is the same tonal problem following the mix everywhere?

Compare several familiar playback systems and a few well-known reference recordings.

If your mix consistently sounds brighter, darker, or muddier than references across several systems, the issue is more likely to belong to the mix.

If the problem only exists in your room: investigate the room before “correcting” the mix for it.

STOP CHASING SPEAKERS

Establish a monitoring reference you can trust.

Listen to several commercial recordings you know well through the same monitoring setup.

Then compare your mix at a similar listening level. You're trying to learn what your room and speakers do to recordings that you already know translate well.

Translation is learned comparatively. The goal isn't for every speaker to sound identical. It's for the important parts of your mix to survive the trip.

FULL MIX / LEVELS

What keeps happening?

RESET THE BALANCE

Stop the fader arms race.

If every quiet instrument gets turned up, eventually everything is loud and nothing is actually clearer.

Pull things back and rebuild the balance around the most important elements of the song.

When something disappears, ask why. “Turn it up” is one answer. It isn't automatically the right one.

THE SONG CHANGES

Your faders are allowed to change too.

A balance that works in a sparse verse may not work when the chorus adds guitars, cymbals, vocals, or other layers.

Use automation when the correct level genuinely changes from one part of the song to another.

Don't force one static setting to solve a moving problem.

DECIDE WHAT MATTERS

Not everything has to be equally obvious.

Decide what the listener should notice first, what supports it, and what can sit farther back.

If every part truly needs constant attention, revisit the arrangement as well as the mix.

A mix is a hierarchy, not a roll call.

FULL MIX / LOUD PLAYBACK

What falls apart when you turn it up?

CHECK AT A SENSIBLE LEVEL

Louder playback changes how you perceive the mix.

Compare the problem at moderate volume rather than making decisions while listening extremely loud.

If particular instruments or frequency areas still become uncomfortable across several systems, investigate those sources specifically.

And protect your ears. Louder isn't a more accurate setting just because problems become obvious there.

CHECK THE FOUNDATION

Find out whether the bass, kick, or room is responsible.

Lower the bass and kick independently and listen for which change actually improves the balance.

Compare another playback system too, especially if the problem is strongest in your mixing room.

COMPARE, DON'T PANIC

Some change with playback level is normal.

Human hearing does not respond to all frequencies equally at every listening level.

Make your important balance decisions at a consistent, comfortable monitoring level and use louder and quieter checks as additional information.

Useful check: Turn the mix very quiet too. What remains obvious when the overall level drops?

KEYS / SYNTH

What's wrong with the keys or synth?

KEYS / BURIED

When does it disappear?

START SIMPLE

Turn it up.

If the part is simply too quiet, fix the level before looking for a processing solution.

Then listen again. If raising it makes something else disappear or makes the mix crowded, you've learned that this isn't only a level problem.

CHECK FOR MASKING

What arrives when the keys disappear?

Lower likely competitors one at a time. Guitars, vocals, other keyboards, and dense effects may be occupying similar space.

Don't automatically brighten the keys. First identify what they're competing with.

THE ARRANGEMENT CHANGED

The solution may only be needed in that section.

Compare the sparse section with the busy one. What instruments or layers enter when the keys disappear?

Automation, arrangement changes, different voicings, or moving a part into another register may solve the conflict without changing the sound for the entire song.

KEYS / TOO BIG

What's making the sound feel enormous?

START WITH LEVEL

Turn it down.

Don't use EQ to solve a fader problem. Lower the part until its basic relationship with the mix feels reasonable.

QUESTION THE SOUND

The preset may have been designed to sound impressive by itself.

Listen to how much low end, high end, stereo width, layering, modulation, and ambience are already built into the patch.

If you're still creating the part, try simplifying the patch before trying to carve space out of it later.

Solo impressive and mix useful aren't always the same thing.

REMOVE THE SPACE

Listen to the dry sound.

Temporarily bypass reverb, delay, chorus, widening, and other spatial effects.

If the mix suddenly opens up, add those elements back only until they're contributing what you actually want.

The patch may already contain effects. Check the instrument itself before adding another layer of ambience in the mix.

KEYS / MUDDY

Is the sound muddy alone or only with everything else?

CHECK THE SOURCE

Start with the patch itself.

If this is a synth or software instrument, see whether you can simplify the sound at the source.

Oscillators, layers, filters, effects, voicing, and release times can all contribute to a dense sound.

Don't automatically EQ around a patch you can still change.

CHECK THE ARRANGEMENT

Look for overlapping parts.

Compare the keys with guitars, bass, vocals, and other sustained instruments.

Try muting parts or changing octaves and voicings before assuming every conflict needs an EQ solution.

Especially with chords: sometimes the cleanest EQ move is playing fewer notes.

KEYS / HARSH

Where does the harshness appear?

CHECK THE PATCH

Can you fix the sound before processing it?

If the instrument is still editable, investigate its filter, oscillator balance, distortion, resonance, effects, and other tone controls.

If you're stuck with audio: identify the area that's actually bothering you and make the smallest useful adjustment.

CHECK THE COMBINATION

The keys may only be one contributor.

Temporarily lower other bright elements and listen again. Cymbals, guitars, vocals, and several synth layers can accumulate into a harsh mix even when each sounds reasonable alone.

DON'T CHANGE EVERYTHING

If only a few moments hurt, treat them like a few moments.

Determine whether a particular note, velocity, synth layer, resonance, or effect is creating the problem.

A targeted change may be enough. Automation or dynamic processing can sometimes solve an occasional problem without changing the entire sound.

KEYS / STEREO

What happens to the stereo sound?

CHECK MONO COMPATIBILITY

Find out what disappears when left and right combine.

Some stereo patches use delay, modulation, polarity, or other differences between the left and right channels to create width.

When those channels are combined, parts of the sound can reinforce or cancel.

Try bypassing width and modulation effects one at a time. Find out what is creating the change before trying to repair it.

QUESTION THE WIDTH

Does this sound actually need the entire stereo field?

Narrow the patch temporarily and listen to what happens to the rest of the arrangement.

If vocals, guitars, or other important parts suddenly have more room, you've identified a useful tradeoff.

Wide isn't automatically better. Width is something you can spend where it helps the arrangement.

ISOLATE LEFT AND RIGHT

Listen to what each side actually contains.

Some stereo patches intentionally place different layers, effects, or modulation on each side.

Listen to the left and right channels separately before assuming the imbalance is accidental.

Then decide: is the asymmetry hurting the mix, or did you only notice it because you went looking for it?

RECORDING

What's going wrong?

Start with what you're actually hearing. We'll work backward from there.

RECORDING / LOW SIGNAL

Where is the signal too quiet?

START AT THE SOURCE

Follow the signal from the beginning.

Make sure the source is actually producing the level you expect, then check the first device receiving it.

Confirm that you're using the appropriate input for the signal you're connecting and that any required preamp gain is being applied.

Don't start at the DAW. If the signal is already weak before it reaches the converter, turning it up later doesn't fix what happened earlier in the chain.

FIND WHERE THE LEVEL CHANGES

If one meter looks healthy and the next doesn't, investigate between them.

Check the signal path between the device showing a healthy level and the DAW input.

Look at routing, output level, input selection, interface settings, and the type of connection you're using.

Useful question: What's the last point in the signal chain where the level looks correct? Start there and move forward one connection at a time.

THIS MAY BE MONITORING

Separate recording level from listening level.

If the recording meters show a healthy signal but your headphones or speakers are quiet, increasing recording gain may be solving the wrong problem.

Check your monitor path, headphone level, control-room level, software monitoring, and any devices between the DAW and your ears.

Two different questions: Is the recording level too low? Or are you simply listening to it too quietly?

STOP ADDING GAIN FOR A SECOND

Find out why the signal started weak.

More gain raises the wanted signal, but it can also make unwanted noise in the signal path more noticeable.

Check the source level, microphone position, input type, cables, connections, and signal path before assuming the answer is simply more gain.

Especially with microphones: if the source can safely be louder or closer to the microphone, improving what reaches the mic may be more useful than continually increasing gain.

RECORDING / CLIPPING

Where does the distortion first appear?

FIX IT WHERE IT HAPPENS

Reduce the level at or before the clipping stage.

If the input or preamp is already clipping, lowering the track later in the DAW won't undo that distortion.

Reduce the level feeding that stage and check whether the source is arriving at the appropriate type of input.

Trace backward. What is feeding this input, and how hot is that signal before it gets here?

TRACE BACKWARD

Find the last clean stage before the DAW.

Check what is feeding the converter or interface input. Look at output levels, preamp gain, routing, and the input you're using.

Pay particular attention when one device is already sending a line-level signal into another device with its own preamp.

Don't fix an input problem with the DAW fader. The signal can already be clipped before that fader ever gets a chance to change anything.

IT MAY NOT BE DIGITAL CLIPPING

Something earlier in the chain may already be distorting.

If possible, listen or meter the signal at different points. The source itself, a pedal, amplifier, microphone, preamp, mixer, or another device may be distorting before the signal reaches the DAW.

Find the first ugly stage. A clean meter at the end of the chain only tells you the level is safe there. It doesn't prove everything before it was clean.

LEAVE SOME ROOM

Check the loudest part of the actual performance.

The average level may look perfectly fine while short peaks are clipping.

Have the performer play or sing the loudest part you're expecting, then leave enough room for the occasional surprise.

You don't need to record right below clipping. A clean recording with room for unexpected peaks is more useful than a hotter recording with accidental distortion.

RECORDING / BLEED

What is the bleed actually causing?

Hearing another source in a microphone is not automatically a problem. First figure out what the bleed is preventing you from doing.

CHANGE THE RELATIONSHIP

Work on what the microphone hears before trying to remove it later.

Listen to the wanted source and the unwanted source separately. Then experiment with their physical relationship to the microphone.

Try changing microphone distance and orientation, moving the sources farther apart, changing their relative playing levels, or using available physical separation.

Perform a small experiment. Move or rotate one thing, record again, and compare. If the bleed improves without hurting the wanted sound, you've learned something useful.

LISTEN IN CONTEXT

The processing may be changing more than the source you wanted to change.

A microphone containing multiple sources does not give your processor a clean way to know which sound you care about.

Compression can make quieter bleed more noticeable. EQ changes anything in that frequency range, including the unwanted sound.

Try less first. Bypass the processing and listen to the microphones together. Then add processing back while paying attention to what happens to both the wanted sound and the bleed.

CHECK THE MICS TOGETHER

The problem may be the relationship between microphones.

The same source can reach different microphones at different times. When those signals are combined, their relationship can change the tone in ways that sound hollow, thin, comb-filtered, or just strange.

Test it. Listen to one mic alone, then add the other. If the sound changes dramatically when they are combined, investigate microphone placement, distance, polarity, and which microphones actually need to be in the mix.

SEPARATION STARTS IN THE ROOM

Change what reaches the microphone.

Try source placement, microphone placement and orientation, distance, relative performance levels, gobos or other useful barriers, and different microphone pickup patterns when appropriate.

Don't chase isolation just because you can. The goal is enough separation to make the recording work, not necessarily perfectly isolated tracks.

WORK WITH WHAT YOU HAVE

Decide whether the bleed actually needs fixing.

Listen to the full recording before attacking individual tracks. Bleed that sounds ugly in solo may disappear into the complete performance or even help the tracks feel connected.

If it really is causing a problem, experiment with editing, automation, gating or expansion, and less aggressive processing on the affected track.

Judge the result, not the solo button. Remove or reduce bleed because it hurts the recording, not simply because you can hear it.

RECORDING / NOISE

When does the noise appear?

ISOLATE THAT PATH

One noisy channel gives you somewhere to start.

Swap one thing at a time with a known-good equivalent: cable, microphone or source, input, and any device in between.

One change at a time. If you replace three things and the noise disappears, you still don't know which one caused it.

LOOK FOR WHAT THEY SHARE

Multiple noisy channels may have a common point.

Look for shared equipment, power, routing, interface connections, computer connections, or another common part of the signal path.

Ask what the noisy channels have in common. A shared problem is often more useful evidence than treating every channel separately.

FIND THE NOISE SOURCE

More gain may simply be making existing noise easier to hear.

Check the source level and microphone placement first. Then isolate devices in the chain to determine where the unwanted sound enters.

Don't diagnose by knob position alone. What matters is the relationship between the wanted signal and the unwanted noise reaching the recording.

YOU FOUND A CLUE

If connecting one device creates the noise, investigate that connection.

Try removing other connections from that device, changing the signal connection, checking its power arrangement, and substituting known-good cables where possible.

Don't defeat protective grounding as a troubleshooting shortcut. Isolate the cause using signal connections and proper equipment rather than modifying mains safety connections.

RECORDING / MULTIPLE MICS

Does each mic sound okay by itself?

COMPARE ALONE VS TOGETHER

Investigate the timing relationship between the microphones.

When multiple microphones capture the same source, differences in arrival time can change the combined frequency response.

Try moving a microphone and recording another short test. Also test polarity reversal if your equipment provides it.

Use your ears, not a rule. Change one variable and compare. You're looking for the relationship that best serves the recording.

FIX THE INDIVIDUAL MIC FIRST

Don't diagnose a multi-mic problem until both signals work alone.

Check that microphone's placement, orientation, input path, cable, gain, and source relationship.

Simplify the problem. Get each individual signal somewhere useful first. Then judge what happens when they are combined.

CHECK MONO

Listen to what happens when left and right are combined.

A stereo recording can sound wide and exciting while hiding relationships that become obvious in mono.

Compare stereo and mono. If important parts of the sound become dramatically thinner or disappear, investigate microphone placement and timing before reaching for processing.

RECORDING / LATENCY

Where are you hearing the delay?

CHECK THE MONITOR PATH

Find out whether you're listening through the computer.

Software monitoring requires the signal to enter the system, be processed, and return to your headphones or speakers. That trip takes time.

If your interface provides direct or hardware monitoring, compare it with software monitoring. If you're using software monitoring, investigate your audio buffer and any processing adding significant latency.

Don't change ten settings at once. First determine which monitoring path you're actually hearing.

YOU MAY BE HEARING TWO PATHS

Check whether direct and software monitoring are both active.

Hearing the immediate signal plus a slightly delayed copy can sound like an echo, doubling, or strange comb filtering.

Test one path at a time. Temporarily disable one monitoring path. If the doubling disappears, you've isolated the problem.

THIS IS DIFFERENT FROM MONITORING DELAY

Check whether the recorded audio itself is misplaced.

Record a simple transient test and compare the recorded event with where you expect it to appear on the timeline.

If the recorded material is consistently offset, investigate interface drivers, DAW recording-offset settings, latency compensation, and the specific monitoring/routing setup.

Measure before compensating. A repeatable offset is much easier to troubleshoot than moving tracks until they feel right.

RECORDING / SOURCE DOESN'T MATCH

When does it stop sounding right?

MOVE THE MICROPHONE

The microphone doesn't hear from your listening position.

Before EQing the recording, move the microphone and make short comparison recordings.

Try changes in distance, angle, height, and position relative to the source.

Treat placement like EQ you can do before recording. Find a position that gives you more of what you want before asking processing to repair it.

FIND THE CHANGE

Compare stages of the signal path.

If the source sounds right at one point and wrong at another, the useful information is somewhere between those two points.

Check input type, gain, processing, routing, converters, and any hardware between the two stages.

Find the last place it sounded right. Then move forward one stage at a time.

CHECK WHAT YOU'RE LISTENING THROUGH

The recording and the playback system are different parts of the problem.

Compare headphones, monitors, and another familiar playback system if one is available.

Also check monitor routing, level controls, accidental processing, and anything inserted on the playback path.

Ask whether the recording changed or your listening path changed. Don't rerecord something until you've separated those two possibilities.

RECORDING / DAMAGE CONTROL

What's wrong with the recording you already have?

DON'T PRETEND IT DIDN'T HAPPEN

First decide how audible the damage actually is.

Lowering the level after recording does not remove distortion that was created earlier in the signal path.

Restoration tools may reduce some audible artifacts, but don't assume they can reconstruct a clean recording perfectly.

If the performance can reasonably be recorded again, compare the cost. Ten minutes rerecording may be better than an hour trying to rescue it.

LISTEN BEFORE CLEANING

How much does the noise matter in the actual production?

Check the recording in context before applying aggressive noise reduction. Processing that removes noise can also change the wanted sound.

Use the smallest intervention that solves the actual problem. Silence between phrases may need editing or automation. Constant noise may require a different approach.

DON'T SOLO YOURSELF INTO A PROBLEM

Listen to the complete performance first.

If the bleed isn't hurting the combined recording, leaving it alone may be the best move.

If it is causing a specific problem, try automation, editing, gating or expansion, and reconsider how heavily the affected track needs to be processed.

Fix a symptom you can actually identify. “There is bleed” is an observation. “The snare in the vocal mic makes the vocal compressor pump” is a problem.

DEFINE "BAD"

Turn the reaction into something you can test.

Is it too dark? Too bright? Too roomy? Too distorted? Too distant? Too noisy? Does the performance feel wrong?

Pick the most obvious symptom first instead of adding processing until something feels better.

Name one problem. A specific problem gives you something to investigate. “It sucks” gives you an extremely accurate emotional assessment and almost no diagnostic information.

MAKE THE BORING DECISION

Compare fixing it with recording it again.

Consider the performance, the problem, how difficult the setup is to recreate, and how much repair the existing recording needs.

If the performance is special or impossible to reproduce, working around technical flaws may be worth it. If the performance is easily repeatable, recording a cleaner version may be the simpler solution.

Don't rerecord automatically, and don't rescue automatically. Keep the version that best serves the music after considering the actual cost of each option.
